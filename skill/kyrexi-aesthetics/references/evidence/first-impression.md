# Evidence · First impression and overall appeal (FI)

How fast first impressions form, what drives appeal at first sight, and how much appeal matters for use. Research entries verified against the papers' text on 2026-11-13; FI-001 is the project's own survey.

### FI-001 · Plain, clear pages beat concept-led pages in early blind pairwise votes
- grade: C
- applies: choosing between a conventional and a concept-led direction
- not-for: audiences unlike the voters (see caveats)
- value: prefer the clear base (TST-001); treat a whole-page concept as a risk to be justified
- source: Kyrexi Aesthetics blind survey, responses to 2026-11-13 (A0 no skill vs A1 Anthropic frontend-design, 6 briefs) · access: Unpublished · reuse: CC BY 4.0
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text (own data)
- caveats: 4 voters, 1 excluded by the attention check; 14 answers from 3 people, all aged 18–34 in India with design or tech backgrounds. Pages without a skill were preferred 13 times, the skill's pages 0 times, 1 tie; reasons most often given: easier to read, clearer next step, more trustworthy. Answers from the same person are not independent, so the real sample is three people.

### FI-002 · Keep pages low in visual complexity and typical for their kind; both shape appeal within a fraction of a second
- grade: B
- applies: every page's first screen and overall layout
- not-for: pages whose brief asks for an experimental or art-led look (then state the trade-off)
- value: aim for low visual complexity and a layout that matches what visitors expect from this kind of site; complexity acts first, typicality catches up within about 50 ms
- source: Tuch, Presslaber, Stöcklin, Opwis & Bargas-Avila 2012, The role of visual complexity and prototypicality regarding first impression of websites · International Journal of Human-Computer Studies · https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf · access: OA (author preprint) · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text (preprint)
- caveats: two lab studies, n = 59 (45 women) and n = 82 (57 women, mean age 27.3), mostly Swiss psychology students; 119 real-site screenshots. Complexity affected beauty ratings at 17 ms; prototypicality's effect was weaker at 17 ms and as strong as complexity's with longer exposure (50–1000 ms). Low complexity + high prototypicality was rated most appealing. Screenshots, not live pages.

### FI-003 · Expect complexity and colourfulness to explain about half of first-impression appeal; the rest is people and content
- grade: B
- applies: deciding how much to rely on visual tuning vs content and audience fit
- not-for: —
- value: tune complexity (AUD-001) and colourfulness for the audience, but do not expect them to carry the page; content, clarity and trust do the rest
- source: Reinecke, Yeh, Miratrix, Mardiko, Zhao, Liu & Gajos 2013, Predicting users' first impressions of website aesthetics with a quantification of perceived visual complexity and colorfulness · CHI 2013 · https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf · access: OA · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text
- caveats: 450 websites, 548 online volunteers; perceived colourfulness model r = .88 (R² = .78); the appeal model with complexity, colourfulness and demographic interactions explains 48% of variance (adj. R² = .48) after 500 ms. Online volunteers, screenshots.

### FI-004 · Do not trade usability for beauty: attractive interfaces improve objective performance only a little
- grade: A
- applies: any conflict between a visual effect and task clarity, speed or accuracy
- not-for: —
- value: clarity and task flow win conflicts; aesthetics is a small bonus on performance, not a substitute
- source: Thielsch, Scharfen, Masoudi & Reuter 2019, Visual Aesthetics and Performance: A First Meta-Analysis · Mensch und Computer 2019 · https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/owms/2019_thielsch_et_al.pdf · access: OA · reuse: ©
- source: Schlamann, Nestler & Thielsch 2026, Attractive Things Do Work Better: A Meta-Analysis on Visual Aesthetics and User Performance (supplementary materials) · Zenodo · https://zenodo.org/records/18777943 · access: OA · reuse: CC BY 4.0
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text (2019); abstract (2026 record)
- caveats: 2019: 25 studies, 101 effects, 3,025 participants, g = 0.12 with high heterogeneity. 2026 preregistered update: 31 studies, 234 effect sizes, 18,794 participants, g = 0.29 with high unexplained heterogeneity; interaction device and typography manipulation moderate the effect. Both are small effects on performance, not on liking.

### FI-005 · Critique visual aesthetics on four facets: simplicity, diversity, colour, craftsmanship
- grade: B
- applies: panel critique (rounds 3 and 5), especially psychologist and devil's advocate
- not-for: —
- value: check each facet separately: simplicity (clear, ordered, balanced), diversity (interesting, not dull), colour (pleasing, fitting palette), craftsmanship (skilful, coherent, up to date)
- source: Moshagen & Thielsch 2010, Facets of visual aesthetics · International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 68(1) · https://www.uni-muenster.de/OWMS/uploads/drafts_thielsch/pdf/moshagen_2010.pdf · access: OA (author manuscript) · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text (manuscript)
- caveats: the facets come from the Visual Aesthetics of Website Inventory (VisAWI, 18 items, seven validation studies); the skill uses them as a critique lens, not as a scored questionnaire.

### FI-006 · Balance typicality and novelty; each helps preference only when the other is held in check
- grade: B
- applies: the base-plus-signature rule (TST-001): a typical base carries the page, one novel moment adds interest
- not-for: art or experimental briefs where novelty is the goal
- value: keep the page's structure typical; add novelty in a contained place rather than everywhere
- source: Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen 2003, 'Most advanced, yet acceptable': Typicality and novelty as joint predictors of aesthetic preference in industrial design · British Journal of Psychology 94 · https://doi.org/10.1348/000712603762842147 · access: PW · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 abstract
- caveats: industrial products (sanders, phones, kettles, cars), not websites. Typicality and novelty are strongly negatively related; their direct correlations with preference were not significant, but each became highly significant with the other partialled out, jointly and equally effective while suppressing each other. Website-specific support for the typical side comes from FI-002.

### FI-007 · Show the offering itself on the first screen, large and close enough to be desired
- grade: B
- applies: any page whose visitors come for something they can see: food, places, products, results, people, events (the offering in the feeling brief, `art-direction.md` 3b)
- not-for: offerings with nothing visible to show (a pure service): use the person or the result instead
- value: the offering appears on the first screen, filling a large part of it, close enough to see its texture and detail, in its true colours; real supplied assets first, drawn stand-ins only at a fidelity that looks real enough to want (light, shine, texture), never flat icons
- source: Spence, Okajima, Cheok, Petit & Michel 2016, Eating with our eyes: From visual hunger to digital satiation · Brain and Cognition 110:53–63 · 10.1016/j.bandc.2015.08.006 · access: OA (Oxford repository) · reuse: CC BY
- verified: 2026-11-14 full text (relevant sections)
- caveats: the evidence is about food: images of desirable food capture attention and trigger appetite responses ("visual hunger"; one cited study found a 24% rise in whole-brain metabolism), mostly from photographs shown in laboratories. Extending the principle to other offerings is an inference; it is consistent with the first-impression rules (FI-001 to FI-006). Never style the offering deceptively (ETH-001, ETH-013).
