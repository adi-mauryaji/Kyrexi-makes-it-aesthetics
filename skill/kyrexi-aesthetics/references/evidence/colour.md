# Evidence · Colour (COL)

Verified against the sources' text on 2026-11-13. Colour research supports associations and harmony rules better than claims that a hue changes behaviour; see `myths.md`.

### COL-001 · Pick hues whose everyday associations are pleasant for this audience; colour preference is learned from what colours remind people of
- grade: B
- applies: choosing the palette's main and accent hues; the culture role checks local associations
- not-for: —
- value: avoid main colours that this audience links with dirt, decay or illness for the subject at hand; prefer hues tied to things they like in that context
- source: Palmer & Schloss 2010, An ecological valence theory of human color preference · PNAS · https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/Palmer%26Schloss%282010%29.pdf · access: OA · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text
- caveats: 48 US participants rated 32 chromatic colours; a single predictor built from how much people liked the objects associated with each colour explained 80% of the variance in average colour preference. Average preferences, one culture; associations differ by culture and group (culture role).

### COL-002 · Build palettes from related hues that differ in lightness; keep strongly contrasting hues for small figure-on-ground accents
- grade: B
- applies: palette construction, backgrounds, cards, the call-to-action colour
- not-for: data visualisation that needs categorical distinctness
- value: harmony and pair preference rise with hue similarity; lightness contrast carries preference; a contrasting hue works as a small figure (button, highlight) on its background, not as an equal partner
- source: Schloss & Palmer 2011, Aesthetic response to color combinations: preference, harmony, and similarity · Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3037488/ · access: OA · reuse: ©
- verified: 2026-11-13 full text
- caveats: lab ratings of colour pairs, not interfaces. Highly contrastive hue pairs were judged neither preferable nor harmonious; figural colour preference rose with hue contrast against the background. No preference advantage for complementary pairs after correction (MYTH-003).

### COL-003 · Use colour to set emotional tone through lightness, saturation and hue together; associations are largely shared across cultures but many-to-many
- grade: A
- applies: matching the palette to the brief's tone (calm, energetic, serious, playful)
- not-for: claims that a colour makes visitors act (trust, buy, hurry): see MYTH-002
- value: light colours read as positive, dark as negative; yellow/orange as positive and high-arousal; blue, green, green-blue and white as positive and low-arousal; red as high-arousal (positive and negative); grey as negative and low-arousal; tune lightness and saturation before hue
- source: Jonauskaite & Mohr 2025, Do we feel colours? A systematic review of 128 years of psychological research linking colours and emotions · Psychonomic Bulletin & Review · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12325498/ · access: OA · reuse: CC BY 4.0
- verified: 2026-11-13 abstract and relevant sections (full text)
- caveats: 132 articles (1895–2022), 42,266 participants from 64 countries; correspondences are systematic and highly comparable across cultures, with local exceptions (culture role). They are associations reported by people, not measured effects on behaviour.

### COL-004 · Let the offering's own colours lead: build the palette around them and never tint or fight them
- grade: B
- applies: palettes for pages whose offering has its own colours (food, fashion, cosmetics, interiors, art, places); fields, overlays and lighting around images or drawings of it
- not-for: offerings without visible colour (a pure service): use COL-001 to COL-003
- value: show the offering in its true colours with no tint, duotone or colour cast; take the palette's dominant and support colours from the offering and its materials; colours that fight it (for example cool or candy fields around warm food) only as small accents, with a reason
- source: Spence 2015, On the psychological impact of food colour · Flavour 4:21 · 10.1186/s13411-015-0031-3 · access: OA · reuse: CC BY 4.0
- verified: 2026-11-14 full text (relevant sections)
- caveats: the review is about the colour of food itself: colour is the most important product-intrinsic cue for expected taste and flavour, a mismatch disappoints, and colour meanings vary by age and culture. That the page around the offering should not distort or fight its colours is an inference, generalised beyond food. The popular claim that warm page colours "stimulate appetite" and blue "suppresses" it found no primary study (2026-11-14 search) and is not to be cited. Origin: the owner's review of three restaurant heroes in pink and blue, mint and indigo that did not make him hungry.
